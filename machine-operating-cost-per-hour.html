Cost per hour

How much does a machine cost per hour to run?

Works for excavators, loaders, dozers, graders, skidders, forestry machines and trucks. The inputs are the same for any machine.

The hourly cost of running a machine, whether an excavator, loader, dozer or truck, is its owning cost (the cost of having the machine, whether it works or not) plus its operating cost (the cost that rises with every hour it runs), divided by the hours it works. It differs for every machine, so this page shows the formula, a worked example with made-up numbers, and a calculator you can fill in with your own.

All figures on this page are in New Zealand dollars and exclude GST.

The formula

  • Depreciation per hour = (purchase price minus expected resale value) divided by expected life in hours
  • Finance and interest per hour = interest rate x average investment, divided by hours worked per year, where average investment = (purchase price + resale value) / 2
  • Insurance and registration per hour = yearly insurance and registration divided by hours worked per year
  • Fuel per hour = fuel price x average litres per hour, where average litres per hour = (share of hours working x working burn rate) + (share of hours idling x idle burn rate)
  • Servicing and repairs per hour = the visitor's own $ per hour figure
  • Tracks or tyres per hour = cost of a set divided by the hours a set lasts
  • Operator per hour (optional) = wage and on-costs per hour; left at 0 if blank
  • Total cost per hour = owning cost per hour + operating cost per hour

    What is included and what is not

    Depreciation is the drop from the purchase price to the resale value you expect, spread over the hours you expect the machine to work. In the calculator, those hours are the hours per year multiplied by the years you keep it.

    Finance and interest is the interest rate times the average of the purchase price and the resale value, then divided by the hours worked in a year. It is a simple yearly interest figure. It is not a lender's repayment schedule.

    Insurance and registration is the yearly amount, divided by the hours worked in a year.

    Fuel is the fuel price times the average litres per hour. The average mixes the working burn and the idle burn by the share of engine hours spent idling. If you leave the idle fields blank, every hour uses the working burn rate.

    Servicing and repairs is your own dollar figure per hour. This page does not estimate it for you.

    Tracks or tyres is the cost of a set divided by the hours that set lasts.

    The operator is the wage and on-costs per hour, and only if you enter it.

    Operator wages, transport, attachments and GST are excluded unless you enter the operator wage. The numbers are only as good as the inputs.

    How much of the fuel bill is idling?

    The idle share is the part of engine hours the machine is running but not doing work. The idle burn is how many litres it uses in each of those hours. Fuel per hour uses both, so you can see how much of the hourly fuel cost is fuel burned while the machine is not working.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is owning and operating cost?

    Owning cost is the cost of having the machine, whether it works or not. It covers depreciation, finance and interest, and insurance and registration. Operating cost is the cost that rises with every hour the machine runs. It covers fuel, servicing and repairs, tracks or tyres, and the operator if you enter a wage.

    Does the cost per hour include the operator?

    Only if you enter an operator wage. Leave that field blank and the operator is left out. The worked example does not include an operator.

    Why use engine hours rather than days?

    Fuel, servicing, tracks, and the life of the machine are counted in hours. Two machines can be on site for the same number of days and work very different hours. Hours worked per year turn the yearly costs into a cost per hour.

    Which machines does this work for?

    Any machine that has a purchase price, working hours and fuel use, including excavators, loaders, dozers, graders, skidders, forestry harvesters and trucks. The method is the same and only the inputs change.
